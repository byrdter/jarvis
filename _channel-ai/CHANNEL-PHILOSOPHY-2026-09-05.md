## 1. The hard part first

> *"AI will fundamentally change everything — the way we work, play, think, relate, learn, build."*

**I think that's true. It cannot be the channel's message.**

Not because it's wrong — because it is the *default thesis of every AI channel that exists.* It is
the wallpaper. And measured, every abstraction of it dies:

Ten confirmations this week, same law every time: **the category dies, the named object with a
present-tense stake lives.** "AI changes everything" is the largest possible category.

### But it doesn't have to be *said* to be *transmitted*

This is the turn, and it's the whole answer to your question.

Wendover has never made a video called *"Logistics Shapes Civilization."* It makes videos about one
port, one airline's fuel hedging, one shipping lane — and after twenty of them the viewer arrives at
the thesis on their own, and believes it, because they built it themselves out of things they were
shown rather than things they were told.

**Your thesis is your editorial policy, not your tagline.** It decides which objects you pick. It
never appears in a title. The viewer should be able to state it back to you after ten videos, having
never once heard you say it.

A thesis you assert is a claim the viewer has to take on faith from a stranger. A thesis they
assemble is one they now own — and defend.

## 3. The philosophy

### One sentence

> **Every video takes one specific claim about AI that somebody is currently making with money
> riding on it, and works out whether the arithmetic holds.**

That is a **method**, not a topic — which is why it survives the fact that the proven AI subject set
is only five things wide (jobs, the bubble, slop, data centers, cognition). Each of the five is a
*site* for the same instrument, not a separate lane you have to win.

### The four commitments

**1. The object, never the category.** Every video is about a named thing with a date: COBOL 1959,
this data center, that filing, this study, that price. Never "AI and society." Ten measurements say
the category has no audience — not a small one, none.

**2. Show the work, and show it failing.** The arithmetic happens on screen. The viewer sees the
numbers, the source, and the step where it goes wrong. This is the part almost nobody does —
Redhawk asserts the lie at 6.78× without ever demonstrating it, which means the position *below* it
is open to anyone willing to demonstrate.

**3. Publish the boring verdict.** *This is the commitment that makes the channel worth anything.*
When you check a claim and it holds up, that video ships too. A channel that only ever finds fraud
is a channel with a thesis it is protecting, and viewers learn to discount it. A channel that
sometimes says "I checked, and they were right" has bought the standing to be believed the next time
it says they weren't.

Nobody in this space does that. It is your single largest available differentiator, it costs nothing
but nerve, and it is authentically yours — you spent this entire week accepting results that
contradicted what you hoped for.

**4. Practitioner, never professor.** Lead with what you can *work out*, never with what you *know*
or where you learned it. Measured three separate ways: "an engineer explains" 11.51× · "a professor
explains" 0.01× · "thirty years in tech" 0.01×. The thirty years shows up in your being *right*,
not in your saying so.

### What this rules out

- No news reaction. No "OpenAI just announced." The peg is a claim, not an event.
- No predictions. You score other people's predictions; you don't add your own.
- No "AI will change everything" video. Ever. It's the thesis; it's not a topic.
- No tutorials, no tool reviews, no dev register — measured, that audience is elsewhere and it is
  not what you want to make.

## 5. How the message actually gets out — the mechanics

You asked how to get people to pay attention. Measured, it's four things and none of them is the
message:

1. **Attention is bought with an object.** The first frame carries a concrete, readable, specific
   thing — a filing, a number, a document. Ten measurements say a thesis in the first frame is a
   dead frame.
2. **It's held with a withheld payoff.** The verdict lands past 40% of runtime, not at 0:30. This is
   already a measured gate in your pipeline (`narrative-measure.py`), and the *AI Doesn't
   Hallucinate* master failed it at 5.6% — so it's a real risk, not a theoretical one.
3. **It compounds when the objects rhyme.** Same instrument, different object, every time. Twenty
   videos of "here's the claim, here's the arithmetic" accumulate into "this person actually
   checks" — which is a reputation, and reputation is the only thing that outruns the algorithm.
4. **Credibility comes from the negative result.** Commitment 3 above. It is the cheapest and rarest
   thing on the list.

**And the honest part:** none of this fixes retention, which is the measured problem on the existing
channel — 93,735 lifetime impressions at a healthy 3.69% CTR, with watch-time per video down 14×
while CTR held. A correct position gets the click. The craft still has to keep them.

## 6. On the name — yours, and one measured input

I'm not picking it. Two things to carry in:

- **The practitioner credential works as a brand.** *Urban Planner Explained* measured 3.67× median
  over 5 hits with the credential in the name. Something in that family — a practitioner stance
  rather than an academic or a personal one — has evidence behind it.
- **The Ordinary Economics rule, learned the expensive way:** a name isn't decided until the handle
  resolves 404 **and** the surrounding namespace is clear. `@ordinaryeconomics` was taken by a real
  channel of that exact name after 96 candidates had been screened, and "Ordinary + financial noun"
  turned out to be contaminated by a real UK investment firm. Check the namespace, not just the
  handle.
