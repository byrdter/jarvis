# Celebrity & Public-Figure Use Checklist (Byrddynasty / "Understanding AI")

**Scope:** any video that names, shows, or discusses a real living (or recently-deceased)
public figure — athlete, founder, celebrity, politician, executive. Run this before scripting
or sourcing images for that beat. Occasional use is fine; this keeps it clean by default.

> Not legal advice. This is an editorial risk-management checklist. For a recurring
> celebrity-content format, get a one-hour read from a media/IP attorney and replace this
> with their bright-line rules. Companion doc: `VISUAL-SOURCING.md`.

---

## The three separate risks (never collapse them)

A "celebrity use" question is really three stacked questions. Sort every use into these:

1. **Copyright — the photo/clip itself.** Owned by the photographer/agency (Getty, AP,
   Reuters), NOT the person. This is what YouTube auto-enforces (Content ID + manual claims).
   Solved by sourcing, not by who's in the frame.
2. **Right of publicity — the person's likeness.** State law; strongest in **CA, NY, TN**.
   Estates of dead stars (Elvis, Prince, Bourdain, Kobe) are aggressively protected. Editorial
   use *about* the person is protected; using a face to decorate/sell is not.
3. **Defamation / false light — imputing wrongdoing.** THE dangerous one. Triggered when the
   story is itself an accusation (fraud, crime, misconduct) against a named living person.
   Has nothing to do with the photo — it's about the *sentence*.

The face is almost never the real problem. **Sourcing** handles #1–2; **precision of
language** handles #3.

---

## GREEN / YELLOW / RED — decide the lane first

**🟢 GREEN — proceed (this is our normal lane):**
- Video is genuinely *about* the person or the event (commentary, news, education, criticism).
- You're reporting **neutral public facts** that already happened ("X was fired," "X hasn't
  signed," "X testified").
- Photo is **licensed editorial / press-kit / Creative Commons / your own**, and credited.
- Every opinion is **attributed** ("the SEC alleged…", "the lawsuit claims…", "critics say…").

**🟡 YELLOW — allowed, but requires precision (escalate care):**
- The story involves an **accusation, lawsuit, or scandal** attached to a named living person
  (e.g. FTX endorsers). Legit news — but one loose sentence turns reporting into an accusation.
  → Apply the **Defamation precision rules** below, line by line.
- The person is domiciled/protected in **CA / NY / TN**, or it's an **estate** of a dead star.
- Finance/investment context with a real named investor (publicity + defamation both in play).

**🔴 RED — stop / rework:**
- Any framing that **imputes culpability** a court hasn't found ("the celebrity who scammed
  investors," "he knew it was fraud").
- **AI-generated likeness** of a real person's face or voice (see AI rule below).
- Face/voice used to **imply endorsement** of the channel, or to decorate unrelated content.
- Photo scraped from Google with **unknown/agency copyright** and no license.

## Sourcing rules (kills risks #1 and #2)

- [ ] Image is **licensed editorial** (Getty/AP editorial), **press kit**, **Creative Commons**
      (check the specific license terms), or **self-made**. Getty *editorial* licenses are cheap
      and are exactly what pro commentary channels actually use.
- [ ] **Credit** is on screen or in the description.
- [ ] Use is **editorial** (illustrating a story about that person) — not decorative, not
      promotional, not implying endorsement.
- [ ] No celebrity face/name in the **thumbnail or title** in a way that implies endorsement or
      fabricates a claim (thumbnails get read as commercial use — extra scrutiny).
- [ ] Not sourced from an untrusted scrape. If provenance/copyright is unknown → don't use it.

---

## Defamation precision rules (kills risk #3 — apply on every YELLOW)

The test: **report what *happened*; attribute every *judgment*.**

- [ ] State **facts and filings** ("X promoted the product," "the product later collapsed,"
      "X was named in the class action"). Facts build the story and are safe.
- [ ] **Attribute every allegation** to its source ("the SEC alleged," "the complaint claims,"
      "prosecutors say"). Never launder an allegation into a stated fact.
- [ ] **Never assert as fact** that a named person *knew, intended, or was complicit in*
      wrongdoing unless **a court actually found that**.
- [ ] Watch the verb: "**promoted**" (fact) vs. "**scammed / was in on it**" (accusation).
      Watch the appositive: "Steph Curry, *one of the people who defrauded investors*…" is a
      buried accusation — cut it.
- [ ] Distinguish **endorsed** from **orchestrated**. Endorsing a thing that turned out to be
      fraud ≠ committing fraud.
- [ ] Remember these subjects are **actively lawyered** on this exact topic. Assume the person's
      legal team will see the video.

**Worked example (FTX celebrity investors):**
- 🟢 "Steph Curry promoted FTX, which later collapsed in fraud. He was named as a promoter in
  the class-action suits."
- 🔴 "Steph Curry, one of the celebrities who helped scam retail investors…"
Same story, same footage — the second sentence is the lawsuit.

---

## AI-likeness rule (fastest-tightening area of the law)

- [ ] **Do NOT use AI to generate the likeness (face or voice) of a real person.** Generated
      likenesses carry *more* risk than a licensed real photo — they hit publicity + false
      endorsement at once, and TN's ELVIS Act (2024), CA AB 1836/2602, and YouTube's synthetic-
      media disclosure + likeness-removal policies specifically target them.
- [ ] For a real person, use a **real licensed image**, not a generated one.
- [ ] If any synthetic media of a real person is unavoidable and cleared, comply with YouTube's
      **synthetic-content disclosure** toggle. (Our channel is *about* AI — being caught faking
      a real person's likeness is uniquely on-brand-damaging.)


If anything lands RED, rework before scripting — don't fix it in the edit.
