# Research Brief

## Research Question

What changed when the grocery shelf became digitally connected, what does the strongest available
evidence say retailers have done with that capability, and which protections address the real risk
without discarding the operational benefits?

## Recommended Thesis

Electronic shelf labels are a real operational advance and not proof of surge pricing. They matter
because they remove the physical delay between a pricing decision and the shelf. The sensible response
is therefore to govern the upstream decision—one public in-store price, no personal-data pricing, a
reasonable stability window, and an auditable change record—rather than treating the screen itself as
the wrongdoing.

The four guardrails are an editorial recommendation, not a claim that existing law universally
requires them.

## Counter-Evidence and Limits

- The direct 114-store study is strong evidence against the claim that ESL installation automatically
  produces surge pricing, but it covers one unnamed chain and is a working paper.
- Walmart and FMI deny current surge/surveillance uses; those are primary statements from interested
  parties, not independent audits.
- FTC evidence concerns pricing intermediaries across industries and does not prove a named grocery
  chain uses ESLs for individualized in-store pricing.
- Consumer Reports documented randomized online price experimentation. It found no evidence that the
  variation was based on shopper demographics, and Instacart said Carrot Tags did not integrate that
  experiment. This is adjacent proof of capability and incentives, not proof against Walmart.
- Fast markdowns can lower food waste and prices. “Dynamic” is not synonymous with “higher.”
- A single public shelf label cannot simultaneously display different prices to two nearby shoppers;
  individualized execution would require another channel, identity mechanism, or checkout/app layer.
