## The Midpoint Reversal

The easy video says the hardware has already produced supermarket surge pricing. The strongest direct
evidence says otherwise. That result must arrive as a genuine correction, not a buried caveat.

The reversal does not end the story because it answers only one question: what happened at one chain
after adoption. It does not answer what adjacent pricing systems can do, what retailers may choose
later, or what protections should exist before misuse.

## The Deeper Turn

The documented grocery-price experiment was online, not on a shelf. This is precisely why the
three-layer spine matters. Consumer Reports found varying prices, but also reported:

- no evidence that demographics caused the differences;
- Instacart's denial that personal data set those prices;
- no current integration between those experiments and Carrot Tags;
- subsequent withdrawal of the price-testing tool after scrutiny.

The consequence is more interesting than “the tag is evil”: technology made a practice possible,
evidence exposed it, and pressure changed the practice. Governance is part of whether an advance stays
an advance.

## Recommended Verdict

**For store workers:** better, if saved time is redirected to useful work rather than used only to cut
hours. Current evidence supports time savings but not a universal labor outcome.

**For shoppers:** better for accuracy, information, and time-sensitive discounts; not better if fast
execution becomes opaque increases or personal-data pricing.

**For retailers:** clearly better execution, with both efficiency and pricing optionality.

**Overall:** keep the labels, govern the decision. The episode recommends four principles:

1. one public in-store price available to every shopper;
2. a disclosed stability window before a price increase takes effect;
3. no use of personal data to raise or individualize essential-goods prices;
4. a retained, auditable price-change history.

These are our editorial criteria for “better,” not a description of universal current law.

## Tone Opportunities

- The price tag got a network connection before most shoppers got an explanation.
- A Kindle-sized screen is receiving blame for decisions made several systems upstream.
- “Dynamic” can mean cheaper bruised peaches at 7 p.m. or costlier umbrellas in a storm; the adjective
  does not supply the verdict.
- The old friction was a worker with a printer and a very long aisle. Removing bad friction is useful;
  deciding what replaces it is the story.

## Do Not Do

- Do not say Walmart currently uses surge, facial recognition, or individualized in-store pricing.
- Do not use online Instacart evidence as proof about physical shelf labels.
- Do not make “dynamic” synonymous with “higher.”
- Do not let the policy section become partisan horse-race coverage.
- Do not pad the history of price tags; the contemporary evidence can sustain the runtime.
